Australian Weather Data Visualisation

Australia’s Rainfall Divide

Rainfall is one of the clearest ways to see how different Australia’s environments can be. A single national average hides a much more uneven story: coastal cities, tropical regions, inland towns, and southern capitals experience rain in very different ways. This visual story uses historical Australian weather observations and city location data to explore where rain falls, when it arrives, and which weather conditions are commonly linked with rain the next day.

1. Rainfall Begins With Geography

The first pattern is spatial. This map places Australian weather locations using matched city coordinates, then compares their average rainfall. Larger circles indicate locations with higher average rainfall, while darker blue tones show stronger rainfall patterns. The purpose of this map is not to predict today’s weather, but to show how strongly rainfall depends on where a place is located.

This immediately shows why rainfall should not be treated as one national story. A person living near a wetter coastal or tropical location may experience rain very differently from someone in an inland or dry-region location.

2. The Extremes Reveal the Divide

After seeing the geography, the next question is simple: which locations sit at the wet and dry ends of the rainfall spectrum? The charts below separate the wettest and driest weather locations, making the contrast easier to compare than on the map alone.

This section is important because it turns the map into a clearer ranking story. It shows that Australia’s rainfall divide is not a small difference between similar places, but a large gap between locations with very different rainfall exposure.

3. Rainfall Is Seasonal, Not Just Regional

Location explains part of the story, but timing matters as well. This heatmap shows how average rainfall changes across the months of the year. Darker cells indicate wetter months, allowing seasonal patterns to be compared across locations.

This helps answer a more useful question for everyday Australians: not just “where does it rain?”, but “when does rain usually arrive?” Some places show a concentrated wet season, while others have rainfall spread more evenly across the year.

4. Explore the Rainfall Calendar Month by Month

The heatmap shows the full year at once, but it can still be hard to follow how rainfall shifts from month to month. This interactive explorer lets the viewer move through the calendar using a slider. Larger bubbles represent higher average rainfall, while darker bubbles show months where rain occurred on a higher percentage of days.

The slider adds interaction with a clear purpose: it lets the audience compare the same locations across different months without changing pages or hiding major sections of the story.

Rainfall spread differs between states

Averages can hide how uneven rainfall is within a state. This box plot shows the spread of monthly rainfall values across weather locations in each state or territory. A taller box means rainfall varies more widely, while points beyond the whiskers highlight unusually wet monthly patterns.

This adds another layer to the story: rainfall is not only different between states, but also uneven within them. Some states contain locations with very different rainfall behaviour, so a single state average can be misleading.

5. Rainfall Also Changes From Year to Year

Seasonal patterns repeat across the calendar, but rainfall also varies between years. This chart summarises average rainfall across the recorded period and shows how rainfall levels changed over time in the dataset.

This should be interpreted carefully. The chart shows variation within the available historical records, not a complete climate-change conclusion. Its role in the story is to show that rainfall is variable across time as well as across place.

6. Frequent Rain and Heavy Rain Are Not the Same Thing

A location can have many rainy days without always receiving large rainfall amounts. Another location may have fewer rainy days but experience heavier rainfall when rain does occur. This visualisation compares rainfall frequency with average rainfall amount, while larger circles highlight locations with more heavy-rain days.

This distinction is useful because “wet” can mean different things. For some places, wetness means regular light rain. For others, it may mean less frequent but heavier rainfall events.

7. Some Locations Are More Often Followed by Rain Tomorrow

RainTomorrow records whether rain occurred the day after a weather observation. This ranking compares locations by the historical percentage of days that were followed by rain.

This is not a live forecast. Instead, it gives a historical view of how often rain followed the recorded conditions at each location. It helps shift the story from “where rain falls” to “where rain is more likely to continue or arrive next.”

8. The Weather Signals Before Rain

Rain is rarely linked to one condition alone. Humidity and pressure are two useful signals because they describe the state of the air before rainfall. Higher humidity often means more moisture is available, while pressure changes can be associated with changing weather systems.

These charts should be read as relationships, not proof of direct causation. They show conditions that were commonly associated with rain the next day in the historical data.

9. Rain Does Not Arrive Equally From Every Direction

Wind direction adds another layer to the rainfall story. The compass-style visualisation shows how wind gust direction is associated with rain the next day. Each spoke points toward a wind direction. Longer spokes indicate directions that were more often followed by rain, while darker blue marks show directions linked with higher average rainfall.

This gives the viewer a more visual way to think about incoming weather patterns. Instead of reading wind direction as a list of labels, the compass layout connects the data to the physical idea of direction.

10. Comparing Wind Directions More Directly

The compass rose gives a visual overview, while this chart provides a more direct comparison across wind gust directions. Together, the two views show the same theme in different ways: wind direction can be part of the conditions associated with rain tomorrow.

This section supports the broader message that rainfall is not only about location and season. Short-term weather conditions also shape whether rain is likely to follow.

11. Final Rainfall Profiles

The final view brings the story together by grouping selected locations into simple rainfall profiles. These profiles combine rainfall frequency, rainfall amount, and rain tomorrow rate, turning several measurements into an easier summary for the viewer.

The main conclusion is clear: Australia’s rainfall risk depends strongly on place, season, and weather conditions. A national rainfall average is useful as a broad reference, but it hides the local differences that people actually experience.
